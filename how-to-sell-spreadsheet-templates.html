How Can I Sell Budget Spreadsheet Templates? The Irregular-Income Niche I Sell In

Spreadsheet templates are one of the few digital products with search demand you can count: “Google Sheets templates” and “budget planner” each draw around 18,100 US searches a month in the Semrush data I work from. But the sellers splitting that demand are mostly selling the same generic monthly budget to people with salaries. I sell a budget system built for the opposite reader — freelancers and gig workers whose income changes every month — and this guide explains how to sell spreadsheet templates by niching that way: the demand, the build quality that justifies $29, and the marketplace question nobody checks until it is too late. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • The demand is documented: spreadsheet and budget-planner searches run at roughly 18,100 a month each (Semrush via KeeperTax) — large enough that a narrow slice of it is a real business.
  • Generic budget planners compete with thousands of near-identical sheets. My product, the Irregular Income Budget System, sells the variation the big sellers ignore: income that arrives in uneven amounts, on uneven dates.
  • A spreadsheet feels worth $29 when it behaves like a small tool, not a decorated grid: instructions tab, protected formulas, sample data, and a dashboard that answers the buyer’s question at a glance.
  • Marketplace eligibility comes first: Etsy is the default answer, but Etsy Payments does not cover every country — I cannot sell there from Bangladesh at all, so I sell through my own store with a Merchant of Record checkout. Check your own eligibility before you build for one platform.

The Demand Is Real — and Generic Is the Trap

Start with the numbers, because they shape the whole strategy. In the buyer-demand data I use for this site (Semrush, via KeeperTax), template searches are among the strongest in the digital-products space: Canva templates at 14,800 searches a month and rising, budget planners and Google Sheets templates both around 18,100. That is the good news. The bad news is visible in any marketplace search: the first pages are a wall of lookalike monthly budget sheets, most of them built for a reader who is paid the same amount on the same day every month. Competing there as a beginner means competing on price and screenshots against sellers with hundreds of reviews. The demand data tells you the category is alive; it does not tell you to enter it at its most crowded point. The wider map of which beginner products have this kind of documented demand — and which are cooling — is in What Digital Products Can a Beginner Actually Sell in 2026? (With Real Search Numbers).

How to Sell Spreadsheet Templates: Niche Down to a Person, Not a Category

The working rule from my own store: pick the buyer whose problem the generic product quietly fails. For budget spreadsheets, that buyer is anyone paid irregularly — freelancers, gig workers, creators, commission earners, small sellers. Their budgeting problem is genuinely different:

  • Income arrives in lumps, so a fixed “monthly salary” cell — the foundation of every generic planner — is wrong from the first row.
  • Good months must subsidise bad ones, which needs a smoothing rule, not just totals: what percentage of a strong month gets held back before it can be spent.
  • Bills are fixed while income is not, so the sheet has to answer “what is safe to spend this week?” rather than “what did I spend this month?”

That is the same niche-down discipline I apply to every product: a specific person with a specific friction beats a broad category with ten times the searches, because the narrow buyer recognises their own problem in the product name. It is the identical logic behind the Canva niches in How Do I Sell Canva Templates? What Buyers Actually Search For — demand sets the ceiling, but specificity is what a new seller can actually reach.

Inside My Product: The Irregular Income Budget System

My living example is my own product, the Irregular Income Budget System, priced at $29 in my store. It is built in Google Sheets — deliberately, because Sheets is free for the buyer, runs on any phone or laptop, and a template link is all I have to deliver. Structurally it does three things a generic planner does not:

  • Income logging by payment, not by month — every payment lands with its date and source, and the sheet builds the month’s real total from actuals instead of hopes.
  • A baseline budget from your worst recent months — fixed expenses are planned against a low-income baseline, so a bad month is survivable by design and a good month produces a surplus the system tells you how to split.
  • A hold-back rule for strong months — a set percentage of above-baseline income is routed to a buffer before lifestyle spending sees it, which is the entire discipline of irregular-income budgeting in one habit.

One disclosure, because this site runs on it: the product is live and the system is the one I built for the income pattern I actually live — but my store’s sales are still at zero, here and everywhere. This article is the strategy and the build, published before the results, and it will be updated with real numbers when they exist. Anyone writing this guide with a sales chart would be showing you someone else’s.

Build Quality: What Makes a Spreadsheet Feel Worth $29

A buyer cannot judge your formulas before purchase, so the perceived quality of a template is set by a short list of craft details — the same list I check before anything enters my store:

  • An instructions tab, written for a non-spreadsheet person. First tab, plain language, three steps: make your copy, enter your bills, log payments as they land. If a buyer needs a YouTube tutorial to start, the product failed.
  • Protected formula cells. Buyers will type into the wrong cell on day one. Lock everything except the intended input cells, so the sheet cannot be broken by the person it was sold to.
  • Sample data included, clearly fake. An empty sheet looks broken; a sheet pre-filled with one example month teaches by showing. Label it as sample data and make it deletable in one motion.
  • A dashboard that answers one question. Mine answers “how much is safe to spend right now?” — big, at the top, updated by the entries below. Decoration is cheap; a correct glanceable answer is the product.
  • Tested as a stranger. Open your own template link in a fresh account, make the copy, and follow your instructions literally. Every seller skips this; every buyer pays for it.

Where to Sell When Etsy Isn’t an Option: Check Eligibility First

Almost every guide on how to sell spreadsheet templates says “open an Etsy shop.” For a large part of the world that advice quietly does not work: new Etsy sellers must be in a country supported by Etsy Payments, and many countries — including Bangladesh, where I am based — are not on that list. There is no workaround worth having; selecting a false country is the kind of shortcut that ends accounts. So my route is the one this site is built around: my own storefront, with checkout handled by Polar as Merchant of Record — Polar charges the buyer’s card, handles the sales tax and fraud side, and pays out to my Bangladeshi bank, none of which I could assemble myself without a PayPal or Stripe account I cannot open. The full setup, including why Gumroad and Payhip failed the same payout test, is in How Can I Sell Digital Products Without PayPal or Stripe? My Polar Merchant-of-Record Setup From Bangladesh.

For readers in eligible countries, Etsy can absolutely be one channel — its built-in search is real traffic. The transferable lesson is narrower: verify you can actually get paid before you build the shop, and keep a direct-store route of your own regardless, because a marketplace account is rented ground. My eligibility problem turned out to be a positioning asset: the whole store, the budget system included, exists specifically for buyers and sellers the default platforms underserve.

Pricing, and the Honest Starting Line

I priced the system at $29 by position, not by hope: far above the $5–$9 generic planners it does not compete with, far below the coaching and courses it substitutes for, and in line with the rest of my store’s ladder. Pricing a template is pricing the problem — a freelancer who avoids one overdrawn bad month has recovered the cost several times over — but the honest footnote matters more than the logic: at the time of writing, that price has produced zero sales, because a listed product with no traffic is a shop with no street. Distribution, not pricing, is my bottleneck, and it is the normal one. Bankrate’s June 2025 survey remains the standing baseline here: median side-hustle earnings are $200 a month and only 14% of side hustlers pass $1,000. Templates are a good product class — documented demand, zero marginal cost, built once — inside an income reality that still starts at zero and climbs slowly.

Bottom Line

Sell spreadsheet templates the way the demand data actually points: not into the crowded centre of “budget planner,” but at a named buyer the centre ignores — in my case, anyone whose income refuses to arrive evenly. Build it like a small tool (instructions, protected formulas, sample data, one glanceable answer), price the problem rather than the file, and settle the marketplace question before the product question: check whether the platform can pay you, in your country, and keep a store of your own either way. That sequence — niche, craft, payout — is the whole guide, and the order is not negotiable.
