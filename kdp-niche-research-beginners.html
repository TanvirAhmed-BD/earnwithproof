How Do I Find a KDP Niche That Isn't Saturated? The Under-1,000-Results Test I Use

Most KDP niche research for beginners is either a paid course or a screenshot of someone else’s royalty dashboard. This is neither. It is the two-number test I run on Amazon before I spend a single hour making a book: demand means the top books for the phrase carry a Best Sellers Rank under roughly 300,000, and low competition means the phrase returns under roughly 1,000 total results. Both numbers are free to check, and together they decided every book in my own catalogue — the four live on my account, and the shelf of finished books queued behind them. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • The test has two halves: top titles with a BSR under ~300,000 (real buyers exist) and under ~1,000 total results for the exact phrase (you are not the ten-thousandth copy).
  • The red-ocean counterexample is one search away: a generic “journal” or “notebook” idea sits inside 30,000+ results — no beginner book survives there.
  • My own catalogue was chosen this way: a large-print Christmas word search ($11.99, live), a dinosaur activity book for kids ($9.99, live), and a four-volume large-print word search series for seniors, built and queued.
  • Passing the test is not a sales promise. My books are live and built to a fixed quality standard — and, honestly stated, my first KDP royalty has not arrived yet. The test picks good ground; it does not fire the starting gun for you.

Why Most Beginners Pick a Niche Backwards

The standard beginner path goes like this: watch a video about low-content books, hear that journals and planners “sell themselves,” spend a weekend making one, upload it, and wait. Nothing happens, because the book entered a category where Amazon shows more than 30,000 competing results for the plain word “journal” alone. The beginner concludes KDP is dead. What actually died was the niche choice — the book never had a shelf to stand on.

The opposite mistake is picking a niche so obscure that nobody searches for it at all. Zero competition feels safe until you realise it usually means zero buyers. A usable KDP niche sits between those two failures: proven demand, thin supply. That is exactly what the two numbers measure, and each one guards against one of the mistakes.

The Two Numbers, Explained

Number one: the Best Sellers Rank (BSR) of the books already selling. Every Amazon product page shows a Best Sellers Rank in its product details — a lower number means the book sells more copies. When I test a phrase, I open the top three to five books and read their BSRs. If the leaders sit under roughly 300,000 in the Books category, real money is moving in that niche: strangers are buying books like the one I am thinking of making. If every top book ranks at 900,000 or worse, the niche is a desert wearing a nice name, and I walk away no matter how much I like the idea.

Number two: the total results count for the exact phrase. Type the phrase a buyer would type — not your book title, the buyer’s words — into Amazon’s search, in the Books department. Amazon reports how many results match. Under roughly 1,000 results means a careful new book can realistically reach the first page or two, where the clicks live. Tens of thousands means the opposite. Between the numbers sits a third, softer signal I use as a tiebreaker: if the first page’s books carry only modest review counts, the incumbents are beatable; if every leader has thousands of reviews, even a low results count can hide a locked door.

The Test, Step by Step

  1. Write the buyer’s phrase, not yours. “Large print word search for seniors” is a buyer phrase. “My amazing puzzle book” is not. Every niche idea gets rewritten as the words a stranger types into Amazon.
  2. Count the results. Search the phrase in Books and note the total. Over ~1,000, the idea is parked or narrowed (add the audience, the occasion, the format — each qualifier cuts the crowd).
  3. Open the top books and read their BSRs. Three to five product pages, BSR from the details section. Leaders under ~300,000 means demand is proven. No leader under ~300,000 means stop.
  4. Glance at the review counts. A first page of books with dozens of reviews is an invitation. A first page of thousand-review fortresses changes the maths even when both main numbers pass.
  5. Ask the only creative question that matters: can I make a visibly better book for this exact buyer — bigger print, more puzzles, a cleaner layout, a proper structure? If the honest answer is no, the niche passing the test still is not mine.

The whole check takes about ten minutes and costs nothing. Compare that with the weeks a full book takes to build, and the order of operations explains itself: test first, build second. The Test on My Own Catalogue

Here is the test applied to books that actually exist, including the ones I rejected:

  • Large-print word search for seniors — passed, and became my biggest project. Buyer demand shows in the ranks of the leading large-print puzzle books, while the phrase-level crowd stays thin next to general puzzle terms. I built a full four-volume large-print word search series on that result; the volumes are finished and queued for upload behind my live titles.
  • Christmas word search, large print — passed, live at $11.99. Seasonal, specific, and aimed at a gift-buyer as much as a puzzler. It is one of the four books live on my account today.
  • Dinosaur activity book for kids — passed, live at $9.99. Children’s educational and activity books are one of the categories where specific still beats generic: one beloved topic, one age band, real activities instead of blank pages.
  • A generic “journal” — failed instantly, never built. The 30,000+ results count is the whole review. Whatever margin exists there belongs to books published years ago.
  • Parent and pregnancy guides — passed on specificity. My six parent books (a week-by-week pregnancy companion, an expectant dad’s guide, a first-year month-by-month, and companions) grew out of the pregnancy channel I run, so the audience was defined before the niche test even ran — the test confirmed the shelf space was there. Alongside them sit an Amazing Mazes book, a travel book and ten adult nonfiction titles, all built, all queued.

One structural note, because families ask: my wife, Nadia Akter, publishes kids’ activity books on her own KDP account — five live, under her own legal, tax and bank details, never the same book on both accounts. Two accounts, two catalogues, one shared rule: every title passes the same two-number test before it is built.

What Passing the Test Does Not Mean

Now the honesty anchor, because this site prints one in every money article. Bankrate’s June 2025 survey (n=2,616 US adults) found the median side hustler earns $200 a month and only 14% earn over $1,000 a month — publishing included. My own position is the floor of that chart: books live, built and queued, and no KDP royalties received yet as this is published. The under-1,000-results test does not change those odds by magic. What it changes is which lottery you are playing: a book on an empty, searched-for shelf gets found and judged on its quality; a book in a 30,000-result swamp gets neither. After the Niche: Make a Book Worth Finding

A niche test gets a book seen; structure gets it kept. Every book I publish follows the same fixed standard: a copyright page, a disclaimer, a table of contents, an introduction, the core chapters or puzzles, a conclusion, a call to action, and an about-the-author page. Low-content does not have to mean low-effort — buyers can feel the difference in the preview, and so can reviewers. The free toolchain and pre-upload checklist behind those books is documented in How Do I Format a KDP Book Without Paid Software? My Free Workflow.

Bottom Line

KDP niche research for beginners does not need software, a course, or anyone’s dashboard screenshots. It needs two free numbers read in the right order: top books selling (BSR under ~300,000) and a thin crowd (under ~1,000 results) — plus the discipline to walk away when either number fails, the way the generic journal fails in one search. Choose ground where a good book can be found, then make the book good. That is the whole method, and it is the one behind every title in my catalogue.
