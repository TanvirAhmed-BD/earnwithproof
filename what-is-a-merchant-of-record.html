What Is a Merchant of Record? The Simplest Explanation for Digital Product Sellers

A Merchant of Record (MoR) is the company that legally makes the sale. When someone buys my $19 captions pack, their card statement and receipt name Polar — my Merchant of Record — not me. Polar charges the card, handles the tax, and later pays my bank in Bangladesh. If you sell digital products and have ever wondered what a merchant of record actually does, or why sellers in countries locked out of Stripe and PayPal rely on one, here it is, explained through my own live checkout. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • A Merchant of Record is the legal seller in the transaction: the platform’s name is on the charge, the receipt and the tax paperwork — not yours.
  • It absorbs the jobs a solo seller normally has to do alone: card processing, sales tax and VAT collection and remittance worldwide, fraud screening, receipts and refund plumbing.
  • The cost is a slice of each sale, not a monthly stack of accounts. On my setup the fee is 5% + $0.50 per sale (plus 1.5% when the buyer pays with a non-US card), with a $10 payout minimum.
  • MoR alone is not enough — the payout still has to reach your bank. Gumroad is also a Merchant of Record and it still failed my test, because it has no payout route to Bangladesh.

The 30-Second Definition

Every sale has one party the law treats as “the seller” — responsible for collecting the right tax, issuing the receipt and standing behind the charge. That party is the merchant of record. When you sell through your own website and your own Stripe account, you are the merchant of record, with everything that implies. When you sell through an MoR platform, the platform steps into that legal role: the buyer’s contract is with the platform, the platform pays you afterwards as a supplier. Same product, same buyer — a completely different pile of paperwork, and it lands on the platform’s desk instead of yours.

What Selling One $19 File Normally Demands of You

Imagine selling a single digital file to a stranger in Germany, the usual way. First you need to accept their card, which means a payment processor account — Stripe or PayPal — approved in your name and country. Then the tax: a digital sale to an EU consumer carries VAT at the buyer’s country rate, and collecting and remitting it is the seller’s legal problem, not the buyer’s; US sales tax has its own state-by-state thresholds waiting behind it. Add fraud screening, chargebacks landing on your account, receipts and refund handling in the buyer’s language and currency. None of this is optional, and none of it is the creative work you started for. For sellers in the US or EU it is merely painful. For a seller in Bangladesh — no PayPal, and no way to open a regular Stripe account in my own name — the normal route is not painful, it is simply closed. That closed door is the entire reason the MoR model matters to me.

What the Merchant of Record Takes Off Your Plate

An MoR platform wraps all of that into one service. Its checkout page takes the buyer’s card. Its systems calculate and collect the correct sales tax or VAT for the buyer’s location and remit it to the right authority. Its fraud screening stands between you and stolen cards, and chargebacks hit the platform’s merchant account, not a personal one you begged into existence. Receipts, download delivery and the first layer of refund mechanics come from the platform too. What you keep is the part you actually signed up for: making the product and bringing the buyer to the page. In exchange, the platform takes a percentage of each sale — which brings us to the honest arithmetic.

What It Costs: My Fee Schedule, Worked on a $19 Sale

My MoR is Polar. My plan’s published fee is 5% of the sale plus $0.50 per transaction, with an additional 1.5% when the buyer pays with a card issued outside the US. Here is a worked example — from that published fee schedule, not from a sale of mine, because my store has not made its first real sale yet (a test purchase passed; real sales are still at zero, and this page will be updated when that changes). A buyer pays $19 for the captions pack with a non-US card: 5% is $0.95, plus the $0.50 flat fee, plus 1.5% ($0.29) for the international card — about $1.74 in total fees, leaving roughly $17.26 credited to my balance. A US card skips the 1.5% and leaves about $17.55. Earnings collect in the account until they pass the $10 minimum payout, then withdraw to my Bangladeshi bank in taka (BDT) via Stripe Connect Express — Polar’s payout rail, which works for me precisely because it is Polar’s Stripe relationship, not one I had to open — with settlement taking about seven days. No monthly fee, no processor account, no tax registration in twenty-seven EU countries. That is the trade, in numbers.

My Own Checkout, Step by Step

Concretely, this is the path a purchase travels in my store. My products live under my Polar organisation, CaptionMint — 12 products priced from $19 to $79, including a $29-a-month subscription (Caption Club), which the MoR bills and renews the same way. Each product has a buy link; mine sit on a small custom storefront page and on a lead page that offers a free sample. A buyer clicks, lands on Polar’s checkout, pays by card, and receives the download from Polar — with Polar named as the seller on the transaction. I get the notification and the balance; the buyer never needed to know or care that the seller behind the product works from Chattogram, and I never touched their card details, their VAT or their receipt. When the balance clears the minimum, it moves to my own bank account in Bangladesh. One system, start to finish, that I was actually allowed to open.

Merchant of Record vs “Bring Your Own Processor”

The alternative model is a storefront that is not the merchant of record: it gives you the shop, but the money runs through a Stripe or PayPal account that you must supply yourself. Payhip works this way — a perfectly good platform that I cannot use, because I have neither account and cannot open them from Bangladesh. The MoR label alone does not save you either: Gumroad has been a merchant of record since 2025, handling tax for its sellers, and it still failed my test at the last mile — its payout options do not include a route to a Bangladeshi bank account, so earnings there would be money I could see and never touch. Creem, another MoR, simply rejected my application. The lesson generalises beyond my country: judge a platform in this order — first, can it pay you; second, is it the merchant of record or must you bring a processor; third, what does it charge. Most comparison posts start at step three. Sellers outside the Stripe/PayPal map cannot afford to.

When a Merchant of Record Is the Wrong Choice

Honesty cuts both ways. If you live where Stripe is available to you, sell at real volume, and can handle (or hire) the tax compliance, an MoR’s roughly 5%-plus per sale is a serious ongoing cost — your own processor at around 2.9% + $0.30 keeps more of every order, and you keep full control of the checkout, the customer relationship and the data. An MoR is also a dependency: the platform can change fees, hold payouts for review, or close accounts under its own policies, and your store inherits that risk. For me the calculation is not close — the alternative to an MoR is not a cheaper processor, it is not selling at all — but a seller with full access to the normal rails should run their own numbers instead of copying mine.

Bottom Line

A merchant of record is the company that legally sells your product, so you do not have to become a one-person payments-and-tax department — or be shut out of selling entirely because your country is missing from a processor’s list. Mine costs about $1.74 on a $19 international sale, pays my own bank from a $10 minimum, and let a 12-product store exist that otherwise could not.
