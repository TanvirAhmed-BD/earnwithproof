What Is the W-8BEN Form? A Plain-English Guide for Non-US Freelancers and Authors

Sooner or later, every non-US earner meets the same confusing screen: a US platform asking you to complete a tax form before it will pay you. If you are asking what is the W-8BEN form for freelancers and authors, here is the short answer: it is a one-page declaration that you are not a US taxpayer, so the platform knows how much tax to hold back from your US-source income. I have filled it in three times myself — for Adobe Stock, inside Amazon KDP’s tax interview, and on Wirestock — and this guide walks through it the way I wish someone had walked me through it. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • The W-8BEN (Certificate of Foreign Status of Beneficial Owner) is how a non-US individual tells a US platform: “I earn this money, I live and pay tax elsewhere, I am not filing a US tax return.”
  • Without it, US platforms generally withhold a flat 30% from US-source royalties and similar income — or simply hold your payouts.
  • Bangladesh has a tax treaty with the US: under Article 12(2), royalties are withheld at 10% instead of 30% when you claim treaty benefits correctly — the claim I made on Adobe Stock.
  • You do not need a US tax number for this form. The foreign tax ID field is where beginners panic: I have no Bangladeshi e-TIN, and where the form allowed it, I used the “not legally required” position — exactly what that option exists for.
  • The form stays valid for roughly three years (until the end of the third calendar year after the year you sign), unless your circumstances change.

Why a US Platform Asks a Bangladeshi for a Tax Form

The logic is simpler than the form looks. US tax law (IRS rules on “withholding agents”) makes the payer responsible for collecting tax on certain payments to foreign persons. When a US company pays royalties, and some other US-source income, to someone abroad, it must either withhold 30% or have paperwork on file proving why a lower rate applies. The W-8BEN is that paperwork for individuals. (Companies and partnerships use the sibling form W-8BEN-E — if you sell as an individual freelancer or author, the plain W-8BEN is yours.)

Two things the form is not: it is not a US tax return (nothing on it asks what you earned), and it is not a bill (signing it costs nothing and owes nothing). It is closer to a signed statement of identity and tax residence that the platform keeps on file for its own compliance. That is why marketplaces build it into onboarding — KDP calls it a “tax interview,” stock sites present it as a form to sign before your first payout — and why refusing to complete it does not make the requirement go away. It just leaves the 30% default, or a frozen payout, in place. This is also why the form matters even when your actual income so far is zero: it has to be on file before the first royalty lands, which is the same plumbing logic behind How Do I Get Paid From Amazon KDP in Bangladesh? What the Form Actually Says, Part by Part

Part I — Identification of Beneficial Owner. Your full legal name, your country of citizenship, your permanent home address (a street address in your own country, not a PO box), an optional mailing address, and your date of birth. Two fields in this part cause most of the beginner stress:

  • US taxpayer identification number (SSN or ITIN). If you have never lived or worked in the US, you almost certainly have neither — and for most platform royalty situations this can be left in favour of the foreign TIN line below. You do not apply for a US tax number just to sell books or stock photos.
  • Foreign tax identifying number (FTIN). This is your own country’s tax ID. In Bangladesh that means an e-TIN — which I do not have. The current form and platform flows anticipate this: where the form allows, you can indicate that you are not legally required to obtain one. Adobe Stock accepted my form on exactly that basis, with the treaty claim doing the real work. What you must not do is invent a number or borrow someone else’s.

Part II — Claim of Tax Treaty Benefits. This is the money section. You name your country of residence (Bangladesh), cite the treaty article, and state the reduced withholding rate and the type of income it covers. For royalties under the US–Bangladesh treaty, Article 12(2) sets the rate at 10% — that is the claim I entered on Adobe Stock, and it is the difference between keeping 90% and keeping 70% of every royalty, forever, until the form expires. Note that treaty rates differ by income type and by country; service fees paid to a freelancer abroad are often outside withholding entirely when the work is performed outside the US, while royalties are the classic withholdable category — which is why this part matters most to authors and stock contributors.

Part III — Certification. You sign (platforms accept a typed electronic signature), date it, and confirm “under penalties of perjury” that the information is true and that you will notify the platform within 30 days if it stops being true — for example, if you move country or become a US tax resident. “Capacity” is simply “Individual” when you sign for yourself.

The Three Times I Have Filled It In

This is the first-hand part, because the form reads very differently once you have actually submitted it:

  • Adobe Stock (signed 2 November 2026). My first time. Bangladesh has no place on Adobe’s direct payout list that avoids this step, and the portal walks contributors through the W-8BEN before earnings can be paid out. No e-TIN, so the FTIN field used the not-legally-required position where allowed, and the treaty claim went in as Article 12(2), 10% on royalties. Accepted — and the account has since had assets go live under that tax status.
  • Amazon KDP tax interview. KDP never shows you a paper form; the “tax interview” is the W-8BEN wearing a wizard. Same questions, same treaty logic, and it has to be completed before royalties can flow through the payment setup described in the KDP royalties guide linked above.
  • Wirestock (submitted, in review). The stock aggregator asked for the same form during payout setup with my Payoneer account. Status: submitted and in review when I last checked — a useful reminder that some platforms review the form manually, so you file it before you need the money, not the day a payout is due.

Three platforms, one form, roughly fifteen minutes each once I understood what it was asking. The Fields That Trip Beginners Up

  • Name mismatch. The name on the form must match the name on the platform account and the documents behind it — same spelling, same order. Most rejections are this, not anything exotic.
  • Address format. Part I wants your permanent address in your country of residence. A platform’s US address, a relative’s address abroad, or a PO box in the permanent-address line can invalidate the treaty claim.
  • Date-of-birth format. US forms use month/day/year. A day/month swap is a silent, stupid way to get a form bounced.
  • Claiming a treaty rate without the article. Writing “10%” without the country, the article (12(2) for royalties) and the income type is an incomplete claim — the platform can default you back to 30%.
  • Waiting until payout day. Some platforms review manually (my Wirestock form sat in review). File during onboarding, while nothing is waiting on it.
  • Forgetting it expires. Diary it: a form signed in 2026 generally carries you to the end of 2029, then platforms start asking again — and a change of country or status restarts the clock immediately.

What the W-8BEN Does Not Do

Three limits, so nobody over-reads this guide. First, it does not register you with any tax authority at home — your obligations in your own country are a separate question this form does not touch. Second, it does not make you a US taxpayer, give you a US tax number, or create any right to work in the US; it does the opposite — it documents that you are outside that system. Third, it is not personalised advice: treaty articles and rates are specific to your country and income type, and if real money is at stake, a local tax professional who handles cross-border earners is worth their fee. My numbers here are one Bangladeshi author’s, shown so you can see the shape of the thing — and the honest footnote is that my royalty income so far is zero, so the 10% rate is, as yet, protecting money that has not arrived. The forms are filed and waiting; that is exactly when you want them filed.

Bottom Line

The W-8BEN is a declaration, not a test: “I am the person earning this, I am not a US taxpayer, here is my country and — where a treaty exists — here is the lower withholding rate I am claiming.” Fill it in during onboarding, keep your name and address consistent with your accounts, cite the treaty article properly (for Bangladeshi royalties: Article 12(2), 10%), and never invent a tax ID to fill a box. Fifteen minutes of paperwork is the cheapest 20% you will ever save.
