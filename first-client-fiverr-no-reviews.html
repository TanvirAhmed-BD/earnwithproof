How Do I Get My First Client on Fiverr With No Reviews?

Every seller starts against the same wall: buyers filter by reviews, and you have none. This is the playbook I am running in real time — my four gigs went live on 1 November 2026, and I will say plainly up front that my first order has not landed yet. What follows is not theory about how to get your first client on Fiverr with no reviews; it is the exact proof-building work I have done so a stranger can judge my work before a single review exists. When the first order lands, this page gets its date and figure added. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • With zero reviews, samples are your reviews. Buyers cannot judge your history, so they judge the work they can see — make sure there is work to see.
  • Your sample portfolio costs nothing if you build it from projects you already own. My gig samples are my own caption packs, my own formatted books, my own CV and my own live websites.
  • Trust built off Fiverr — published books, a live product store, real websites — can be pointed to from your gigs and closes much of the no-reviews gap.
  • The market rewards proof more than ever: Fiverr’s active buyers fell about 22% in a year while spend per buyer rose to $368 — fewer, choosier buyers ordering bigger jobs.
  • Your first “order” may be a scam. Mine was: a fake buyer with an external link and an empty dashboard. The dashboard is the only truth.

Why “No Reviews” Is a Proof Problem, Not a Talent Problem

A buyer comparing two gigs is not really comparing skills — they are comparing risk. The seller with 200 reviews feels safe even if their work is ordinary; you feel risky even if your work is better, because nothing about you can be verified. You cannot argue your way out of that gap with adjectives (“professional, high-quality, fast”). You close it with evidence: samples that show the finished product, a portfolio that exists outside one platform’s walls, and offers so specific that the buyer can picture the delivery. Everything below is one of those three moves, in the order I did them.

Step 1: Manufacture Samples From Work You Already Own

New sellers stall here because they think a portfolio requires past clients. It does not — it requires finished work, and you are allowed to be your own first client. My four gigs were each stocked this way. The captions gig carries sample caption packs I wrote for my own content pages, formatted exactly as a client would receive them. The resume gig’s sample is my own CV rebuilt as an ATS-friendly resume with its cover letter, before and after. The KDP formatting gig shows a chapter from one of my own published books, formatted with the trim size, margins and table of contents a buyer would get. The website gig links to live one-page sites I built for my own projects — a buyer can click and see the deliverable working, not a mock-up. Total cost: zero. Total excuse left: none. Whatever your skill is, produce three finished specimens of it, for yourself, this week, and your gig stops looking empty.

Step 2: Borrow Trust From Outside Fiverr

Fiverr is not the only place your reputation can live, and for a new seller it should not be. I am a published author on Amazon with live books, I run a store with 12 digital products, and the site you are reading exists because I build things. None of that is a Fiverr review — but a buyer who finds a real author page, a real store and real websites stops wondering whether I am a real person who finishes work. Practically: mention your outside work in your gig description and FAQ where it is relevant, keep your profile description consistent with it, and when you send a custom offer, reference the specific outside proof that matches the buyer’s job (“the formatting sample in my gallery is from my own book, live on Amazon”). Verified existence is the closest substitute for a review count that a beginner can build.

Step 3: Work Buyer Briefs Like a Job Interview

Briefs — the requests buyers post that Fiverr matches to sellers — are the one channel where a no-reviews seller competes on the message, not the rating. My rules for them, learned from studying the requests that land in my own inbox: reply as fast as you reasonably can, because buyers read early replies; mirror the buyer’s own words back — their business type, their deadline, their exact deliverable — so the offer reads as written for them alone; ask one sharp question that proves you understood the job; and price the first job at your entry tier, not at a desperation discount. What never to send: the copy-pasted “dear sir, I am expert, order me” paragraph. Buyers can smell it, and in a year when buyers are fewer and spending more per order, the specific, evidence-carrying reply is the one that gets read twice.

Step 4: Make the Gig Itself Answer the Trust Question

Your gig page has about ten seconds to answer the buyer’s real question — “can I trust this person?” — so design it backwards from that. Gallery images should show the deliverable, not decoration: a page of finished captions, a formatted book spread, a live site screenshot. The first two lines of the description should name the buyer and the outcome before they mention you. And the packages should make the safe choice easy: a small, clearly-scoped starter tier is what a first client is actually buying. The Trap I Already Met: The Fake First Order

On 1 November 2026 — the day my gigs went live — a message arrived from an account calling itself amella_42476, presenting what looked like an order and pointing me to an external link to proceed. It felt, for about ten seconds, like the fastest first sale in history. It was not an order. My Fiverr dashboard showed nothing — no order, no payment, nothing — because the entire thing was designed to move me off the platform, where the “verification” or “fee” step would follow. I reported and blocked the account. Burn this rule in: if it is not inside your Fiverr dashboard, it does not exist. No real client needs you to click away, pay to verify, or chat privately before an order exists — and new sellers with no reviews are exactly who these scripts hunt, because the hunger for a first order is strongest in week one.

Where I Honestly Am in This Timeline

So: four gigs live since 1 November 2026, samples built from my own work, briefs answered with specific offers, one scam refused — and zero orders so far. That is not a failure state; it is the documented normal one. Seller blogs that rank for this question cluster around a two-to-six-week wait for a first order on a well-set-up gig, and I track my own clock against that consensus, week by week, in How Long Does It Take to Get Your First Order on Fiverr? An Honest Timeline. The wider baseline agrees: Bankrate’s June 2025 survey (2,616 adults) found the median side hustle earns $200 a month and only 14% of side hustlers pass $1,000 a month — figures earned by people who stayed past the silent first weeks, not by people who found a shortcut. My job in those weeks is the work above, done repeatedly, not a new hack every day.

Bottom Line

You get a first client with no reviews by removing every reason a buyer has to guess: samples they can inspect, outside proof they can verify, brief replies written for one person instead of everyone, and a gig page that answers the trust question in ten seconds. Refuse the fake shortcut when it arrives — it will arrive dressed as your first order — and let the real one come through the dashboard.
