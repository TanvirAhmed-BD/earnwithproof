What Digital Products Can a Beginner Actually Sell in 2026? (With Real Search Numbers)

Ask ten gurus which digital products to sell for beginners and you get ten recycled lists. I started from the other end: the search numbers that show what buyers actually type into Google when they are ready to pay, then matched those numbers against the 12 products sitting in my own store. Some of my products line up with real demand. A few popular categories are quietly cooling — and the same data shows that too. Here is the full picture, good and bad. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • Buyer demand is real and measurable: Semrush data (via KeeperTax, October 2026) puts “Canva templates” at 14,800 searches a month and rising, and “budget planner” at 18,100 a month.
  • The winning pattern is a big category niched down to one buyer — not “budget planner” but “budget planner for freelancers whose income changes every month”. That is literally one of my products.
  • Seller hype is cooling even while buyers remain: searches for “digital products to sell on Etsy” fell about 75% in a year, and Notion template demand fell about 45%.
  • My store’s honest status: 12 products live, a test purchase passed, no sales yet. The products match the demand data; the missing piece is traffic, and no list can hide that.

Start With Buyer Searches, Not Seller Hype

Two very different crowds type digital-product phrases into Google. Buyers search for the product itself — “budget planner”, “Canva templates”, “digital planner” — usually with a card in hand. Sellers search for the business idea — “digital products to sell”, “how to sell printables”. Most beginner advice is written for the second crowd, which is why it all sounds the same. The numbers below are Semrush search volumes for the United States (published by KeeperTax in October 2026), cross-checked with DataForSEO figures published by Craftific, and they describe the first crowd: people looking for a product to buy. One caveat before the list: US databases undercount the rest of the world, so treat these as a reliable ranking of demand, not a census.

Canva Templates: 14,800 Searches a Month, and Rising

Templates are the strongest beginner category in the data. “Canva templates” pulls 14,800 US searches a month on Semrush’s count — 22,200 on DataForSEO’s — and Semrush marks the trend as rising, which is rare in this list. The demand is not for one giant generic bundle; it fragments into social-media template sets, business and marketing templates, media kits, resumes and event stationery, each bought by a specific kind of customer. That fragmentation is the beginner’s opening: you do not need to out-template Canva’s own library, you need to make the exact pack one niche reaches for weekly. My own products lean this way — caption and content packs built for specific professions rather than “10,000 templates for everyone”. How that niche-down process works, and what buyer phrasing to copy, is the whole subject of How Do I Sell Canva Templates? What Buyers Actually Search For.

Spreadsheets and Budget Planners: The Quiet 18,100

The least glamorous category posts some of the biggest numbers. “Budget planner” sits at 18,100 searches a month and “Google Sheets templates” at 18,100 as well — people with a money problem, searching for a tool that solves it this week. Spreadsheets also age well: a good budget sheet does not depend on a design trend. The research pattern here is identical to templates — the head term is competitive, the niched version is the play: budget planner for couples, paycheck budget planner, and my own corner of it, the Irregular Income Budget System, built for freelancers and gig workers whose income lands in lumps instead of a salary. It is priced at $29 in my store and exists because I live the problem it solves. I break down the niche, the build and the listing in How Can I Sell Budget Spreadsheet Templates? Digital Planners: Smaller Numbers, an Easier Fight, a Seasonal Peak

“Digital planner” is smaller — 9,900 searches a month — but its keyword difficulty (KD 29 on Semrush’s scale) is the friendliest on this page, which matters more than raw volume when you are new and unknown. Planners are also sharply seasonal: demand climbs toward roughly 18,100 a month across December and January (DataForSEO figures), when New Year organisation intent peaks. That is why planner content belongs live in November, not in a January panic. A simple planner for one defined user — a student planner, a shift-worker planner, a content planner for small creators — is a realistic first product: buildable in free tools, easy to demo with screenshots, and aimed at a buyer who searches every single year.

Digital Stickers: Tiny Volume, Tiny Competition

At the small end, “digital stickers” records about 1,000 searches a month at a KD of just 11 — the lowest competition figure in the KeeperTax set. Nobody gets rich on sticker packs, and that is not their job. Their job is to be a complete first rep: make a product, list it, watch a real checkout work, learn how delivery and refunds feel, all in a weekend. Beginners routinely skip this step and start with the hardest category instead; the data suggests starting where the fight is smallest and climbing.

The Cooling Shelf: What the Same Data Says to Avoid

Now the part the listicles skip. Seller-side interest is falling off a cliff: Semrush shows searches for “digital products to sell on Etsy” down from about 1,900 to about 470 a month — a drop of roughly 75% — and Notion template demand down about 45%, with printable wall art down by half. Read that correctly: buyers have not vanished (the buyer phrases above are steady or rising), but the 2024–25 gold rush of sellers copying each other is ending, and undifferentiated products are what is being left behind. Two more traps hide in the same table: “ebook” shows a tempting 27,100 searches a month but a KD of 94, meaning the results are owned by giants; and generic low-content products — the blank journal, the undated planner for no one — compete against tens of thousands of near-identical listings. If a category needs you to be the ten-thousandth copy, it is not a beginner category, whatever the screenshots on social media claim.

My Own Store, Checked Against the Numbers

So how does my shelf score? My store (run under the name CaptionMint) holds 12 products priced from $19 to $79: a social-media captions pack at $19, an AI prompt pack for a named profession at $39, a monthly Caption Club subscription at $29, a set of “business OS” workflow packs between $49 and $79, the Irregular Income Budget System at $29, and planner and family-organisation products at the lower end. Against the data: the spreadsheet and planner products sit squarely in proven buyer demand; the template-style packs match a rising category; and I deliberately have no generic journal, no Notion clone and no “10,000 prompts for everyone” pack — the three cooling traps above. What the numbers cannot do is sell anything by themselves. The store is live, the checkout passed a real test purchase, and the sales counter is still at zero — the products match demand, and now they need the slower work of being found. The platform underneath the store matters as much as the products: I sell through a Merchant of Record because the famous alternatives cannot pay a Bangladeshi bank account, a setup I walk through in How Can I Sell Digital Products Without PayPal or Stripe? A 20-Minute Test for Your First Product

Before you build anything, run three checks. One: do buyers search for it by name? Find the phrase in a keyword tool or even in Google’s autocomplete, and prefer steady buyer phrasing over seller phrasing. Two: can you name the single buyer in one sentence — “a realtor who posts daily”, “a freelancer paid irregularly”? If the buyer is “anyone”, niche it down until it is someone. Three: can you make a genuinely good version with free tools this week? A finished small product teaches you the checkout, delivery and support loop; a perfect product that never ships teaches nothing. Pass all three and you have a beginner product worth listing — then price it like the ladder in my store, low enough to start, high enough to respect the work.

Bottom Line

Beginners can sell digital products in 2026 — the buyer searches prove the demand is there — but the easy-copy era is over, and the money sits in specific products for specific buyers: templates and planners niched down, spreadsheets that solve a money problem, small low-competition items to learn on. Keep the baseline in view while you choose: Bankrate’s June 2025 survey (2,616 adults) puts the median side hustle at $200 a month, with only 14% of side hustlers clearing $1,000 a month — and my own store, products live and sales at zero, is what the honest start of that curve looks like. Pick the product the numbers and your skills agree on, ship the small version first, and let proof accumulate.
