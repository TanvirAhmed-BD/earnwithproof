How Do I Get Free Backlinks for a New Blogger Blog? (No Paid Tools, No Buying Links)

If you are searching how to get free backlinks for a new blog, you have probably already seen the two bad answers: spam directories, or a $5 gig promising 500 links. I know, because my older blog carried posts on exactly this topic — they picked up 22 to 33 views each, and precisely zero useful backlinks. This article is the version written after I stopped reading about backlinks and started building them: the small, unglamorous network of links I actually control — my own websites, my profiles, my answers on other platforms — what each one cost (nothing but time), and what they have honestly done so far. No paid tools, no bought links, and no claim that a handful of links has made anything rank yet. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • The first backlinks a new blog can realistically get are from assets you already control: your other sites, your channel, your profiles. Mine form one small interlinked network — a custom website, a Blogger blog, a store and lead page, a YouTube channel, Quora and LinkedIn profiles — built entirely for free.
  • Buying links is the one shortcut I refuse: Google’s spam policies treat purchased links as link spam, and the bulk packages sold to beginners are exactly the pattern that gets new sites buried. A new blog has nothing to spare for that risk.
  • What the links have done so far is unglamorous but real: new pages get discovered and indexed fast because Google keeps arriving from pages it already knows. Ranking movement is a later chapter, and this post will be updated when there is movement worth reporting.

What Counts as a Backlink Worth Having

A backlink is any link from another page to yours. Google reads links as votes, but the votes are not equal: a link from a real, relevant page that a human maintains counts; a link from an auto-generated directory page that lists ten thousand blogs counts for nothing, and a link bought in a bulk package can count against you. For a brand-new blog, the practical definition is simpler: a backlink worth having comes from a page you would be happy to show a reader. That test quietly rules out almost every “free backlink site list” circulating in beginner forums — those lists are the same forty directories everyone submits to, which is precisely why they carry no weight. The methods below pass the test because each link sits inside something real: a website I run, a profile that is genuinely mine, or an answer I actually wrote.

The Backlinks I Built First: My Own Network

The fastest legitimate links are the ones nobody can refuse you, because they are yours. Over the past months I have ended up running a small constellation of free properties, and linking them together — where the link genuinely helps a visitor — was the first backlink work I did:

  • My main website links to my blog, and back. My pregnancy brand lives on a custom site (hellobumpandbloom.com) with a Blogger companion blog. The website’s pages point readers to blog articles; the blog’s sidebar and posts point back to the website’s guides. Two properties, each making the other discoverable.
  • My store and lead pages. My digital-product store and my free-sample lead page are separate free sites, and each links to the content that explains the products — and the articles link back to them. When EarnWithProof launched, it joined the same pattern: every property links where a reader would naturally want to go next.
  • My YouTube channel. The channel’s About section and video descriptions carry links to the website and blog. Video descriptions are real pages on a domain Google crawls constantly, and the links are exactly where a viewer finishing a video would look for more.
  • My profiles. My Quora profile, LinkedIn profile and Amazon author page each carry the relevant website link in the fields those platforms provide for it. Profile links are usually nofollow — they pass little ranking weight — but they are followed by people, and they tell Google these properties belong to one real person, which is its own kind of proof.

None of this required outreach, tools or money. It required only noticing that a beginner who runs more than one free property is already sitting on their first backlink portfolio.

Answering Questions Where Readers Already Are

The second method is slower and more valuable: go where the questions are, answer them properly, and link only when the link genuinely extends the answer. My working example is Quora. I keep a dressed profile there, answer two or three questions a week in the topics I actually work in — pregnancy publishing, KDP, freelancing — and my first five answers each carry one link to a relevant page on my website, placed where a reader who wants the full guide would want it. The rules I hold myself to: the answer must stand on its own without the link, the link must match the question, and I never drop links into other people’s threads as a drive-by. Forum and community links built this way rarely pass much ranking weight either. What they pass is readers — real ones, arriving with the exact problem your article solves — and for a new blog, early readers matter more than early “authority”.

Becoming Worth Linking To (The Slow Lane, Started Early)

Every backlink guide eventually arrives at the same sentence: publish things other people want to cite. It is true and it is slow, so the only useful advice is to start the asset-building before you need the links. My version of a linkable asset is the thing this whole site is built on: real numbers from real work — an actual fee breakdown, an actual timeline, an actual screenshot-shaped fact like “102 pageviews in four days, almost all of them mine”. Generic advice gets paraphrased; specific proof gets linked. I also do small, manual outreach to blogs in my niches when a new article genuinely adds to something they published — a few personal emails, not a blast. Honest status report: this lane has produced conversations, not a pile of links yet. It is in this article because it belongs in a complete method, not because it has paid off. When it does, the numbers land here.

What I Refuse to Do: Buying Links

The temptation is real, because the offers are everywhere: hundreds of backlinks, “high authority”, delivered in days, for the price of a coffee. Here is why I will not touch them. Google’s spam policies name buying or selling links that pass ranking credit as link spam, and the bulk packages sold to beginners are built from the same recycled networks Google has been discounting and penalising for a decade. For an established site, a bad link purchase is a mess to clean up. For a new blog, it can be the whole story — the domain starts its life under suspicion, and I would be paying for the privilege. The same logic covers “free” link exchanges and comment-spam tools: anything whose pitch is quantity is selling the one thing links are not. My entire backlink count today would fit in this paragraph’s first sentence, and it is worth more than a spreadsheet of five hundred bought ones.

What the Links Have Actually Done So Far

Time for the proof section, which on this topic means modest numbers stated plainly. The measurable effect of my little network is discovery speed: when EarnWithProof launched on 7 November, Search Console verification, sitemap submission and the first indexing requests all happened the same evening, and the first URLs were accepted for indexing within hours — because Google was not finding an orphan site in the dark; it was following paths from properties it already knew. My older properties show the same pattern in reverse: the blog’s traffic sources in its first days included my own website sending readers across. What the links have not done yet is move rankings in any way I can honestly report — the sites are days to weeks old, and anyone who tells you their week-one backlinks transformed their rankings is selling something. Links compound with content and time; the content side of that equation is the 40-article batch this post belongs to. For the machinery that turns discovered pages into indexed pages, see my Search Console indexing checklist; for what to do when an indexed post still will not rank, see the 9 fixes I ran on my own posts.

Bottom Line

Getting free backlinks for a new blog is mostly not a hunt — it is housekeeping. Link your own properties together properly, put your website in every legitimate profile field you own, answer real questions where your readers already gather, and start publishing the kind of specific, numbers-first articles that give other writers a reason to cite you. Refuse the bulk packages, however cheap: on this topic, the free-and-slow road is also the only safe one. Expect the first payoff to be faster indexing and a trickle of real readers, not rankings — and if you want the honest timescale for the rest, it is laid out with my own first-week numbers in How Long Does It Take for a New Blog to Get Traffic?.
