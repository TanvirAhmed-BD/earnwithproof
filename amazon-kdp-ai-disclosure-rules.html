Do I Have to Tell Amazon I Used AI in My KDP Book? The Disclosure Rules, Explained Simply

Short answer: yes, if an AI tool generated any of the text, images or translations in your book — the Amazon KDP AI content disclosure rules require you to declare it during upload, even if you edited every line afterwards. If AI only assisted your own work, you do not. I publish on KDP myself — four books live and a much larger catalogue built and queued — and the disclosure screen is part of every upload I make. This is exactly how I answer it, book by book. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • KDP asks the question directly during title setup, in three parts: AI-generated text, AI-generated images, and AI-generated translations.
  • “Generated” means an AI tool created the content. Heavy editing afterwards does not change the answer — Amazon’s rule looks at where the content came from, not how much you polished it.
  • “Assisted” means the content is yours and AI only helped you check, edit, organise or brainstorm. AI-assisted work needs no disclosure.
  • On my own books I declare the parts AI generated (draft text, generated images and interiors) and answer “no” where AI only proofread or organised my own writing. Disclosure has never blocked or penalised one of my uploads.

What the Amazon KDP AI Content Disclosure Rules Actually Say

Since September 2023, Amazon’s KDP content guidelines have required authors to inform Amazon when a book contains AI-generated content. The distinction the whole rule hangs on is between two words that sound similar and mean different things. AI-generated content is text, images or translations created by an AI-based tool — Amazon’s definition applies even when you substantially edit the output afterwards. AI-assisted content is content you created yourself, where AI tools were used to edit, refine, fact-check, format or brainstorm around your own work. The first must be disclosed. The second must not — Amazon does not ask you to report it, and ticking “yes” for purely assisted work would actually misdescribe your book.

The rule applies to Kindle ebooks and paperbacks alike, and it is not optional or cosmetic. Disclosure is part of KDP’s content policy, which means answering falsely is a policy breach, not a paperwork slip. At the same time, disclosing is not an admission of anything shameful: Amazon sells enormous numbers of books whose setup records declare AI-generated content, including, in the relevant parts, mine. The checkbox is a record, not a scarlet letter — as of my own uploads, nothing about the declaration appears as a label or badge on my books’ product pages.

Where the Questions Appear in Your Own Upload

You meet the questions inside the title setup flow, on the content side of publishing, alongside the manuscript and cover upload. Amazon asks whether your book contains AI-generated content, broken into the three categories — text, images, translations — so you answer for each part separately rather than giving one blanket answer for the whole book. A book can honestly be “yes” for images and “no” for text, or the reverse, and mine often are. Sitting near the same part of the flow is a separate question about low-content books (journals, planners and similar). That one confuses beginners because the two questions arrive together, but they are unrelated: low content describes the type of interior, AI disclosure describes how the content was made.

The questions take thirty seconds if you arrive knowing your answers — which is the entire point of reading this before you upload. Rushing it is how people tick the wrong box: not from dishonesty, but because they never worked out which side of the generated/assisted line their own book sits on.

The Line Amazon Draws — With Examples From My Own Catalogue

The working test I use is one question: who produced the first version of this? If an AI tool produced the first version of anything the reader will read or see, it is generated. If I produced it and AI only helped me improve it, it is assisted. Applied to real parts of my own catalogue:

Generated — I declare it. First-draft chapter text for my nonfiction titles that came out of an AI writing tool before I rewrote, expanded and restructured it — generated text, declared, because editing does not change its origin. Puzzle and activity content produced with software and AI help for my word-search and kids’ activity books — declared. Cover or interior artwork produced by an image generator — declared under images. If I ever publish an edition translated by an AI tool, that will be declared under translations.

Assisted — no declaration. My own drafted pages run through an AI grammar and clarity check. AI suggestions for titles and subtitles that I chose between and rewrote. AI help organising my own notes into the book structure I use for every title — copyright page, disclaimer, table of contents, introduction, chapters, conclusion, call to action, about the author. In each case the words and the work originated with me; the tool was an editor, not an author.

The grey zone is real, and here is my rule for it: when I genuinely cannot decide whether a section counts as generated, I disclose it. The declaration costs nothing, takes seconds, and has no visible effect on the book. Non-disclosure, if Amazon later judges the content to be AI-generated, risks the book or the account. Asymmetric risks deserve the safe answer.

What I Actually Tick on My Own Uploads

Across my four live books — children’s activity books including a dinosaur activity book and a large-print Christmas word search — and the catalogue queued behind them (a four-volume word-search series, ten adult nonfiction titles, six parent books), my answers follow the pattern above rather than a single blanket policy. The nonfiction uploads declare AI-generated text, because their first drafts came from AI tools before my editing. The activity books declare the AI-generated content inside their interiors. Where a book is entirely my own writing with AI used only for checking and organising, I answer no — because that is the truthful answer for that book. My wife publishes on her own, strictly separate KDP account (five books live) and applies the same test to her own uploads; two accounts, two catalogues, the same rule.

One honest observation from doing this repeatedly: no upload of mine has been blocked, delayed beyond the normal review, or visibly treated differently because of a “yes” answer. The review wait my books went through is the standard one every KDP author gets. Whatever fear surrounds this screen is larger than the screen deserves. The risky behaviour is not using AI — half the new catalogue does, which is exactly why Amazon built the question. The risky behaviour is lying on a form Amazon can audit, for the sake of a checkbox nobody outside your account can see.

What If You Already Published and Answered Wrong?

Fix it rather than unpublishing in a panic. From your KDP bookshelf you can edit a live book’s details and content, correct the disclosure answers to the truthful ones, and resubmit; the book goes through review again, which is normal and survivable. If the content itself is compliant and only the declaration was wrong, a prompt correction is a paperwork fix. Amazon’s enforcement is aimed at deception and at prohibited content, and its terms allow removing books or closing accounts for policy violations — which is precisely why correcting early and voluntarily is the sensible move. Treat it like correcting a typo in your tax paperwork: boring, quick, and much better done by you than discovered by them.

Bottom Line

The disclosure rules reduce to one honest habit: declare what AI generated, stay silent about what it merely assisted, and decide before you reach the screen using the first-version test. Do that and the most feared step in modern KDP publishing takes thirty seconds.
