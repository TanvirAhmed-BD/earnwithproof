How Do I Format a KDP Book Without Paid Software? My Free Workflow

Formatting is where most first-time KDP authors assume they need to pay someone — or buy software like Vellum or Atticus. I format every one of my own books with a toolchain that costs $0, including 133-page paperback interiors, full wrap covers and a four-volume large-print word search series. This guide shows exactly how to format a book for KDP for free: the tools, the settings that matter (trim size, margins, table of contents), and the pre-upload checklist that catches my own errors before Amazon’s reviewers do. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • My full toolchain is free: a word processor (Google Docs or LibreOffice Writer) for the interior, Canva’s free tier for the cover, and Amazon’s own free cover template and Print Previewer for checking.
  • The settings that decide pass or fail are boring and knowable in advance: trim size, inside margin that grows with page count, bleed on or off, and a spine width calculated from your page count.
  • Every book I publish follows one fixed structure — copyright page, disclaimer, contents, introduction, chapters, conclusion, call to action, about the author — so formatting starts from a checklist, not a blank page.
  • I also sell this as a Fiverr service ($30/$60/$100) — and I’ll say plainly below that client orders for it are still at zero. The workflow earns its keep on my own catalogue first.

How to Format a Book for KDP for Free: The Toolchain

Paid formatting software sells speed and pretty templates. It does not sell compliance — Amazon’s requirements are public, and a free tool that exports a clean PDF meets them just as well. Here is what I actually use, book after book:

  • Interior: Google Docs or LibreOffice Writer. Both handle the three things a KDP interior needs: custom page size, mirrored margins, and a table of contents generated from heading styles. LibreOffice exports a print-ready PDF with fonts embedded; Docs works fine for text-driven books.
  • Activity pages: the same documents, built carefully. My word search puzzles, solutions and large-print pages are laid out in documents and tables, not in specialist puzzle software — slower, but free, and I control every cell.
  • Cover: Canva (free tier). A KDP paperback cover is one flat image — back cover, spine, front cover — at exact dimensions. Canva’s custom-size canvas handles it; the free tier is enough because the maths comes from Amazon, not from the design tool.
  • Checking: Amazon’s free tools. KDP’s cover template generator gives you an exact-size template for your trim and page count, and the online Print Previewer shows your files the way the print system sees them. These two catch more real errors than any paid tool I’ve seen recommended.

The publishing flow these files feed into — manuscript, cover, metadata, review — is laid out step by step in How Do I Publish My First Book on Amazon KDP for Free? (Step by Step, With Canva). This article is the formatting layer underneath it.

Step 1: Lock the Trim Size and Margins Before Anything Else

Every formatting disaster I have seen starts here: writing the book first and choosing dimensions later. Decide these numbers before page one, because changing trim size afterwards means rebuilding the file.

  • Trim size. My nonfiction books are 6×9 inches, the standard trade size readers expect. Activity and workbook-style books — the word searches, the journals and planners — are 8.5×11 inches, because large print and puzzle grids need the room.
  • Inside (gutter) margin grows with page count. KDP’s minimum inside margins: 0.375 in for 24–150 pages, 0.5 in for 151–300 pages, 0.625 in for 301–500 pages. My 133-page interiors sit in the first band; I set the inside margin a little above the minimum so text never feels swallowed by the binding, and I always use mirrored margins so left and right pages alternate correctly.
  • Outside margins. At least 0.25 in on the top, bottom and outside edges; I use 0.5 in for a comfortable read. If any content runs to the page edge (bleed), add 0.125 in to the page size on the outer edges and keep essential content inside the safe zone.
  • Page numbers and running heads. Front matter (copyright, contents) carries no page numbers; numbering starts at the first chapter. Section breaks — not manual spacing — are how you keep that clean.

Step 2: Build the Interior in One Fixed Order

I hold every book — mine or, one day, a client’s — to the same structure standard, because readers and reviewers both notice what’s missing:

  1. Copyright page — title, author, year, rights line.
  2. Disclaimer — especially on any advice-adjacent topic.
  3. Table of contents — built from heading styles, never typed by hand (see the next step for why).
  4. Introduction — what the book does and who it is for.
  5. Core chapters/content — one heading style for chapter titles, one for sub-headings, applied ruthlessly consistently.
  6. Conclusion — the wrap-up readers actually remember.
  7. Call to action — an honest one: review request, series link, author page.
  8. About the author — short, factual, human.

For the word search series the “chapters” are puzzle sets and the solutions sit at the back — the skeleton is the same. Ten nonfiction books and four word search volumes have gone through this exact order, which is also why formatting a new volume is now a half-day job instead of a week of improvisation. Choosing which books deserve that effort is a separate skill, covered in How Do I Find a KDP Niche That Isn’t Saturated? The Under-1,000-Results Test I Use.

Step 3: The Table of Contents — Generated Last, Never Typed

A hand-typed contents page is a promise your page numbers will break. Every edit shifts pagination, and the contents page quietly starts lying — readers notice, and it reads as amateur in the Look Inside preview, which is where paperback sales are won and lost. The free workflow avoids this entirely: style every chapter title with the same heading style as you write, then let the word processor generate the table of contents from those headings after the final edit, and export the PDF immediately after. No retyping, no drift. One related rule from my own uploads: generate the TOC, export, and then don’t touch the text again — if you must edit, regenerate. It takes two minutes and it is the cheapest quality insurance in the whole process.

Step 4: The Full Wrap Cover, and the Thin-Spine Lesson

A paperback cover is a single PDF: back cover + spine + front cover, in that order, sized to your trim and your page count, because the spine width is calculated from the number of pages and the paper you chose. KDP’s free template generator gives you the exact dimensions and shows the safe zones — keep text and faces inside them, keep the barcode area on the back clear, and export at 300 DPI.

Here is the error this step caught in my own catalogue. One of my shortest books runs to 49 pages, which makes a spine of about 0.11 inches — a few millimetres. My first cover file for it carried spine text, the way longer books do. At that width, spine text is not a design choice; it is a printing defect waiting for a rejection or a misprint. The fix costs nothing: short books ship with a plain spine — no text — and the template check is where you confirm the spine width before you design, not after. Any book of mine under roughly 80 pages now gets the no-spine-text treatment automatically.

Step 5: The Pre-Upload Checklist That Catches My Errors

Before any book goes up, both files go through Amazon’s Print Previewer and this list:

  • Previewer shows zero red flags — content outside margins, bleed mistakes and low-resolution images all surface here, before a human reviewer ever sees the book.
  • Page count matches everywhere — the interior PDF, the cover template and the metadata must all agree, because the cover was built for one specific count.
  • Fonts embedded in the exported PDF (LibreOffice does this on export; check the PDF properties, don’t assume).
  • Spine rule checked — text on the spine only if the page count gives it room.
  • TOC regenerated after the last edit, and the Look Inside pages read once, slowly — that preview is your shop window.
  • AI-disclosure answers ready. KDP asks what AI generated versus what it merely assisted; I keep the per-book answers with the book’s files. The rules are explained in Do I Have to Tell Amazon I Used AI in My KDP Book? The Disclosure Rules, Explained Simply.

The Honest Limits — and the Paid Version of This

Free tools have real costs; they are just paid in time instead of money. Canva’s free tier limits some elements and exports, a word processor will never match dedicated software for complex illustrated layouts, and a 133-page interior built by hand takes me hours a template-driven tool might halve. For a beginner publishing their first books with no budget, that trade is obviously right. It stops being right when your time is worth more than the software — I am not there yet, and this site’s rule is to say so.

Because I run this workflow constantly, I offer it as my third Fiverr gig — KDP formatting at $30/$60/$100 depending on length and complexity. The standing disclosure: client orders so far are zero. The gig is live, the portfolio samples are my own real books, and the first order has not landed yet — the same position I describe across this site, because the proof has to come before the pitch. And the baseline for all of it stays Bankrate’s June 2025 survey: median side-hustle earnings of $200 a month, with only 14% of side hustlers clearing $1,000. Formatting skill is an asset that compounds across every book; it is not a lottery ticket, and treating it that way is exactly why the free route is the correct route at the start.

Bottom Line

You do not need paid software to format a professional KDP paperback. You need the dimensions decided first, one fixed book structure, a generated (never typed) table of contents, a wrap cover built on Amazon’s own free template, and a pre-upload checklist you actually run — including the discipline to leave spine text off thin books. That is the whole of my workflow, the one behind every book in my catalogue. Paid tools buy back time later; at the beginning, they only buy back mistakes a checklist prevents for free.
