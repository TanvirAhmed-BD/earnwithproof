How Can Bloggers Make Money Without AdSense? 5 Routes I Use Myself

Ask how to make money blogging without AdSense and most answers are theory from blogs that quietly run AdSense. This one is a description of an actual setup — mine. I run two blogs: Bump & Bloom, a pregnancy blog on Blogger that supports my YouTube channel and my books, and EarnWithProof, the site you are reading. Neither uses AdSense. Instead, five routes do the monetisation work: my freelance gigs, my digital products, my books, disclosed affiliate links, and stock content — held together by an email list. And the number that frames all of it, stated before anything else: blog income so far is $0. The pregnancy blog recorded about 102 pageviews across its first days, most of them launch traffic — my own visits and announcement clicks, not search readers. This article is about why the stack is built this way anyway, and why for a small new blog it beats display ads even before it has earned a cent. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • Display-ad maths on a new blog is brutal: at a few hundred pageviews a month, AdSense-style earnings round to nothing. One sale of a $19 product or one small gig order is worth more than months of early ad impressions.
  • My five routes: Fiverr services linked from relevant posts, digital products sold through my own Polar-powered store, KDP books the blogs exist to support, Amazon Associates affiliate links (disclosed on the page, every time), and Adobe Stock content earnings — plus an email list on Brevo collecting the readers ads would have rented.
  • The only display advertising anywhere in my setup is on the Blogger blog, and it is deliberately gentle: Adsterra native banners and a social bar — no popunders, no forced redirects. It has earned $0 so far; it is a placeholder, not a strategy.
  • Honest status: two blogs live, five routes wired, $0 earned from blogging to date. The Bankrate June 2025 baseline — median side-hustle earnings of $200 a month, only 14% above $1,000 — is what normal looks like at the start.

Why AdSense Is Not in My Setup

AdSense pays for attention by the thousand: impressions and clicks priced in cents, meaningful only when traffic is large. A new blog has the opposite profile — tiny traffic, but readers who arrived with a specific problem and are unusually ready to act on a good answer. Selling that reader a fraction of a cent of advertising is the worst available use of them. There is also a fit problem: the Bump & Bloom audience is pregnant women reading on phones, often anxious, and the aggressive formats that make small-site display ads pay anything at all — popunders, interstitials, redirect tricks — are exactly the experience I will not put in front of them. So AdSense was never applied for. The design question became: what can a blog with 100 pageviews sell that is worth more than 100 pageviews of ads? The answer is anything with a price on it — which is what the five routes below are.

How to Make Money Blogging Without AdSense: The 5 Routes Running on My Blogs

  • Route 1 — Services: the blog as a shop window for gigs. EarnWithProof’s articles are written from the same work I sell on Fiverr — captions, resume writing, KDP formatting, one-page websites. A reader who trusts a guide about gig descriptions is a plausible buyer of a formatting service, and the path from article to gig page is one honest link, not a sales funnel. Services are the fastest of the five routes because they need one buyer, not a crowd.
  • Route 2 — My own digital products. The guides point to the products they grew out of: templates, planners and prompt packs sold through my own store, with Polar as Merchant of Record charging the card and handling the tax — the setup detailed in How Can I Sell Digital Products Without PayPal or Stripe? My Polar Merchant-of-Record Setup From Bangladesh. A single $19–$49 sale outearns a season of beginner ad impressions, and the store launched to exactly zero followers — that starting condition is documented in How Do I Sell My First Digital Product With No Audience and No Investment?.
  • Route 3 — Books: the blog is the support act, and that is fine. Bump & Bloom exists in an ecosystem: a YouTube channel publishing week-by-week pregnancy videos, and KDP books — pregnancy companions, kids’ activity books — that the same audience buys in print. Every blog post is a companion to a video and carries the books with it. A blog that reliably moves even a few book sales a month has done more than an ad-covered blog ten times its size.
  • Route 4 — Affiliate links, disclosed and restrained. On the pregnancy blog, product recommendations link through the Amazon Associates programme, and every page carrying them says so — the disclosure is part of the post, not fine print. The restraint matters as much as the disclosure: products are recommended where they genuinely belong in the guidance, and the family rule is never to buy through our own links. Affiliate income so far: $0 in earnings — clicks exist, sales have not followed yet, and the route stays because its cost is honesty plus a link.
  • Route 5 — Stock content and gentle ads, the slow background routes. My stock portfolio on Adobe Stock earns independently of the blogs, and the blogs are where the lessons from it get published — content routes compound each other. The one display-ad element, Adsterra’s native banner and social bar on the Blogger blog, sits here too: switched on, format-restricted, and at $0 earned, a placeholder proving the point of this article — at this traffic level, ads are decoration.

Underneath all five sits the asset that makes them grow: an email list (Brevo, double opt-in, a free sample as the signup offer). A blog’s search traffic belongs to Google’s mood; a list belongs to the blog. Every route above converts better to a reader who has agreed to hear from you again than to a stranger who arrived once.

What the First Real Numbers Actually Look Like

Since this site’s promise is numbers, here are the real ones. The pregnancy blog’s first days produced roughly 102 pageviews in total, and I know exactly what most of them were: launch announcements, my own checking, clicks from the new website — the statistical signature of a blog nobody has found yet, not an audience. EarnWithProof is younger still. Blog income across both: $0. Gigs: live, first order pending. Products: live, first sale pending. This is not the embarrassing part of the story that gets skipped — it is the normal shape of month one, and the Bankrate baseline (median $200 a month among people who do earn; only 14% above $1,000) says the normal shape stays modest for a while. What the numbers argue for is the stack design: with traffic this small, the only routes with a chance are the ones that pay per action — a gig enquiry, a product sale, a book royalty, an affiliate commission — rather than per thousand glances. How long the traffic itself takes to grow is a separate, equally unglamorous question, and it is answered with this blog’s own data in How Long Does It Take for a New Blog to Get Traffic? My First Months, With Real Numbers.

Bottom Line

A small blog makes money without AdSense by not trying to sell its traffic at all — it sells the things the traffic came for: the service behind the guide, the product the guide grew from, the book the channel supports, the occasional honestly-disclosed affiliate recommendation, and the slow background routes like stock. Wire all five, collect the email addresses, and let AdSense be a decision for the traffic level where it would actually pay rent. My blogs have earned $0 so far, and I would build them the same way again — because the first dollar, when it comes, will arrive through a route that pays dollars, not fractions of cents. When it lands, this page gets the update, with the amount and the route it came through.
