How Can I Sell Digital Products Without PayPal or Stripe? My Polar Merchant-of-Record Setup From Bangladesh

You can — if the platform itself acts as the seller of record. I sell digital products from Bangladesh through Polar, which takes the buyer’s card payment, handles the sales tax and fraud checks as Merchant of Record, and pays my Bangladeshi bank account through Stripe Connect Express. I have never needed a Stripe or PayPal account of my own, because from Bangladesh I cannot properly have either. Below: the setup exactly as I did it, the fees in plain numbers, and the two popular platforms that failed my payout test before Polar passed it. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • The blocker for sellers in countries like Bangladesh is not making products — it is getting paid. PayPal does not support receiving here, and a regular Stripe account is not available to Bangladeshi individuals.
  • A Merchant of Record sells on your behalf: it processes the card, handles VAT/sales tax, screens fraud, and pays out to your bank. My payout runs via Stripe Connect Express to my Bangladeshi bank — Polar’s rail, not a Stripe account I opened or manage.
  • Gumroad failed my test (no payout route to a Bangladeshi bank account); Payhip failed it too (it pays out only through your own Stripe or PayPal — the two accounts I cannot have).
  • My store is live and approved with 12 products priced from $19 to $79; Polar’s free plan takes 5% + $0.50 per sale, with a $10 minimum payout.

The Real Problem: Getting Paid, Not Getting Products

Anyone can make a digital product in a weekend. A caption pack, a planner, a template — the file is the easy part. The wall appears at checkout. A buyer in the US or Europe wants to pay by card; that money has to pass through a payment processor and land, legally, in your bank account. For a seller in the US that chain is invisible. For a seller in Bangladesh it breaks at the first link: PayPal does not offer receiving and withdrawal here, and Stripe does not onboard Bangladeshi individuals for a regular account. Every platform that says “just connect your Stripe or PayPal” is, without meaning to, saying “not available in your country.”

So the question “how do I sell digital products without PayPal or Stripe?” is really a question about who stands in the middle. The answer that works is a Merchant of Record — a platform that becomes the legal seller for the transaction. It charges the buyer, remits the taxes, absorbs the fraud risk, and then pays you, the creator, as a supplier. If you want the concept explained slowly, read What Is a Merchant of Record? The Simplest Explanation for Digital Product Sellers after this one.

How to Sell Digital Products Without PayPal or Stripe: Test the Payout Page First

Before Polar, I tested the two platforms every beginner article recommends, and I tested them in the order that matters — payout page first, product page later.

Gumroad. Simple, famous, and its 10% fee is fair. But its payouts go to bank accounts in a supported-country list that does not include Bangladesh, and it offers no Payoneer, Wise or wire-transfer route I could use. A store I cannot withdraw from is not a store; it is a display window. Gumroad failed the only test that counts.

Payhip. The free plan’s 5% fee looks better, until you read how you get paid: Payhip does not pay you itself. Your money flows through your own connected Stripe or PayPal account. For me that is the same wall in a different colour — the two accounts I cannot have. Payhip failed the test too.

The lesson is the method, not the brand names: for any platform, verify a complete payout path to your own bank account, in your own name, before you upload a single product. Screenshots of other people’s dashboards do not count. Your country, your bank, your name.

My Setup, Step by Step

Here is the sequence I actually followed on Polar, in order:

  1. Created the organisation. I registered an organisation called CaptionMint, set up as an individual seller, USD pricing, digital downloads, one-time products (plus one subscription, the Caption Club).
  2. Verified my identity. Polar’s onboarding asked for identity verification, which I completed with my own documents. My dashboard status reads “Identity verified” — this step is not optional, and it is also your protection: the account, the products and the payouts are all provably mine.
  3. Connected the payout. Polar’s payout onboarding runs through Stripe Connect Express. This is the part people misunderstand, so plainly: it is Polar’s payout rail, opened inside Polar’s flow, with my Bangladeshi bank account as the destination. It is not a regular Stripe account that I applied for, manage, or could use for anything else. Bangladesh is on the supported payout-country list, and the money arrives in my own bank in taka.
  4. Built the products. Twelve products are live, priced on a ladder from $19 to $79 — a caption pack at $19, planners and systems in the $24–$49 range, and a full business OS at $79. Each product gets its own Polar buy link.
  5. Put the buy links on my own storefront. I built a simple store page and placed the Polar links there, plus a free-sample lead page that feeds it. Polar handles the checkout; my page just has to be findable and honest.
  6. Submitted for review — and tested a purchase. The store was approved, and before promoting anything I ran a test purchase on my own $19 captions pack to prove the full loop works: checkout, payment, delivery, and the balance appearing in my dashboard. If you skip this step, your first customer becomes your test — a bad trade.

The Fees and Payout Terms, Plainly

Polar’s free (Starter) plan takes 5% + $0.50 per sale; cards issued outside the US add 1.5%. There is no monthly fee. Payouts are manual withdrawals with a $10 minimum, on roughly a 7-day settlement rhythm. Worked example, because percentages hide the truth: on a $39 product, 5% is $1.95, plus the $0.50 fixed fee makes $2.45 total, so $36.55 is what the sale contributes toward my next payout (before any international-card surcharge). Compare that honestly with my Fiverr maths, where a $100 order becomes about $80 before withdrawal costs — platform percentages are a cost of distribution, and the only question is whether the platform earns it. A platform that also handles global VAT, fraud screening and chargebacks is doing real work for its cut.

What I Sell — and the Honest Status

The twelve live products are the working assets of my other projects, productised: caption packs and AI prompt packs from my CaptionMint work, a realtor client system, a freelancer money-and-clients system, a budget system for irregular income (built because my own income is irregular), planners and organisation kits. The price ladder is deliberate: a $19 entry product a stranger can risk, mid-tier systems at $39–$49, and one $79 flagship.

And the status, stated the way this site states everything: the store is live, approved, and checkout-tested. Traffic is the work in progress — the products are linked from my storefront, my lead page and my profiles, and I am building the audience with guides like this one. Bottom Line

If your country locks you out of PayPal and Stripe, do not waste months trying to borrow, fake or work around an account — that road ends in frozen balances. Sell through a Merchant of Record instead, verify the payout reaches your bank before you build anything, test one real purchase, and then spend your energy where it belongs: on products and traffic. My store runs that way today, from Chattogram, with zero accounts I am not allowed to have.
