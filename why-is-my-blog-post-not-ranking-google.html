Why Is My Blogger Post Not Ranking on Google? 9 Fixes I Tried on My Own Blog

Every new blogger eventually types the same frustrated question: why is my blog post not ranking on Google? I typed a version of it while staring at my own first blog’s numbers — a best post with 84 views, SEO articles sitting at 22 to 33, and days with no views at all. So I ran a proper diagnostic on my own posts instead of guessing, and this article is the result: the nine checks I now run, in the order I run them, each one illustrated by a real post of mine that failed it. Two honest notes before the list. First, several of my “underperforming” posts turned out to be failing checks so basic they are embarrassing — which is exactly why they come first. Second, my newest site is days old, so some fixes below are verified by coverage status changing, not by ranking jumps; where a result is still pending, I say so, and this post gets updated as Search Console data fills in. Published November 2026 · by Tanvir Ahmed.

Key takeaways

  • Most “not ranking” posts fail at step zero: on my pregnancy blog, 60 of 65 posts were scheduled, not published — and a post that is not public cannot rank at all. Check publication and indexing before touching the content.
  • The next most common cause is target selection: a new blog cannot win head terms (“how to make money online” is searched ~135,000 times a month at very high difficulty), and simple questions get answered by Google’s AI Overview before any blog is clicked.
  • The fixes that remain are unglamorous and cumulative: retarget the post to a specific low-difficulty question, expand thin content with first-hand detail, wire in internal links, and then give a young domain the one ingredient no fix replaces — time.

Fix 1: Confirm the Post Is Actually Published

This check exists because of my own dashboard. My pregnancy blog holds 65 posts — of which, when I checked, only 5 were published. The other 60 were scheduled for future dates, deliberately, as part of a long content runway. From the inside, the blog felt full; from Google’s side, it was a five-post blog. Any of those 60 “underperforming” posts would have scored exactly zero in search no matter how good they were, because they did not publicly exist. Before diagnosing anything else, open the post in a private browser window while logged out. If you cannot see it as a stranger, neither can Google.

Fix 2: Confirm It Is Indexed

Published is necessary; indexed is the next gate. Paste the post’s URL into Search Console’s URL Inspection tool. If the verdict is not “URL is on Google”, ranking is impossible and content tweaks are wasted effort — fix the indexing first. My full routine for that lives in my Search Console indexing checklist: sitemap in place, indexing requested, internal links so the crawler can reach the page. Only move to Fix 3 when inspection says the page is in the library and merely not being chosen.

Fix 3: Check Whether the Keyword Is Winnable at All

A brand-new blog with no authority walking into a head term is a featherweight entering a heavyweight bout. The flagship phrase in my niche, “how to make money online”, draws roughly 135,000 searches a month at a difficulty score in the 90s — the first page belongs to institutions. My older blog’s experience proves the point from the other side: its best-performing early post, with 84 views, was a question-title post on a giant topic — and 84 views is what “winning” looks like when the topic is too big: a trickle, not a ranking. The fix is retargeting: one specific question, the kind of phrase a real person types with a real situation attached — a platform, a country, a constraint, a number. My working rule for this site is to aim at the low-difficulty band (roughly KD 0–20) until Search Console shows the site earning impressions somewhere. Rewrite the post around the narrower query rather than abandoning the work: the narrower question is usually hiding inside the broad one you wrote first.

Fix 4: Check Whether an AI Overview Answers the Question Without You

My older blog carried standalone grammar Q&As — clean, correct, and nearly invisible. The reason is structural: when a question has a short factual answer, Google’s AI Overview increasingly answers it directly in the results, and industry measurements show position-one clicks falling by more than half on such queries. Nobody is punishing those posts; the click they were built for no longer exists. The fix I applied is consolidation: language help survives on EarnWithProof only inside money-making assets — grammar and clarity fixes appear inside my gig-description guide, where a reader needs them to earn, not as standalone answers competing with an AI summary. If your post answers a question Google now answers itself, fold its content into a bigger, experience-based article and let that article carry the traffic.

Fix 5: Check Whether the Post Is Too Thin to Be the Best Answer

Short is not automatically thin — but my early posts were both. A few paragraphs of general advice, no numbers, no examples, nothing a reader could not get from ten identical pages. Google has thousands of near-identical answers to choose from and no reason to choose a new domain’s copy. The fix is to add what only you can add: your figures, your timeline, your screenshots described in words, your mistakes. Every article in this site’s current batch was rebuilt on that rule — a fee post works a real $100 example down to what lands in a bank account; a timeline post states my actual launch dates and my actual zero orders. First-hand specifics are simultaneously the reader’s reason to stay and the algorithm’s reason to rank you above the paraphrases.

Fix 6: Check for Missing Internal Links

An orphan post — published, indexed, linked from nothing — starves twice: crawlers reach it rarely, and readers never flow into it from your stronger pages. Auditing my older content, the pattern was obvious: posts published whenever they were finished, linked whenever I remembered, which was not often. The fix is procedural, not inspirational: every new article here ships with two or three internal links to named related posts, appears on its category page and the homepage list the day it publishes, and older related articles get a link back to it within a day. This cluster you are reading is built that way deliberately — the backlink, indexing and traffic articles all point at each other, because a cluster tells Google what your site is about, and topical focus is a ranking asset a new blog can actually control.

Fix 7: Check Whether the Title Matches How People Actually Search

Readers do not search in essay titles; they search in questions and fragments. My posts that earned their early views share a shape — plain question titles that mirror the query word for word (“How do I make money online without spending money?”), while the cleverer titles earned nothing. The fix costs ten minutes: retitle the post as the exact question it answers, put the target phrase in the first paragraph and in one subheading, and make sure the title promises only what the article delivers — a mismatch there shows up as clicks that bounce, which teaches Google to rank you lower next time.

Fix 8: Check That the Post Targets One Thing

Early on I wrote posts that drifted — one article trying to cover backlinks, ranking and traffic in a few hundred words each. A drifting post competes with itself: Google cannot tell what it is the best answer to, so it becomes the best answer to nothing. The fix is the discipline this batch runs on — one post, one target keyword, one reader question, answered completely, with everything else split into its own article and connected by links. If your audit finds a post covering three topics, you have not found one weak article; you have found three unwritten ones.

Fix 9: Accept the Fix That Is Just Time

The last check is the one beginners resist: sometimes nothing is wrong. A new domain with modest links, publishing on low-competition questions, can do everything in Fixes 1–8 and still watch posts sit at zero for weeks — my zero-view days happened on a blog that was, mechanically, doing most things right. Google extends trust gradually as a site publishes consistently and accumulates signals; the honest consensus among people who grow blogs for a living is that meaningful search traffic is a three-to-six-month project, and I laid out my own first-week numbers against that curve in How Long Does It Take for a New Blog to Get Traffic?. The practical fix for the time problem is sequencing: spend the waiting months on the posts and links that will compound, and on the small set of legitimate links described in my free backlinks guide — so that when the trust arrives, it arrives to a site that is ready for it.

Bottom Line

When a post is not ranking, work the list in order and stop at the first failure — published, indexed, winnable keyword, safe from instant answers, substantial, interlinked, properly titled, single-purpose, and old enough. On my own blogs, the failures clustered at the top of that list, which is good news disguised as embarrassment: the early fixes are the cheap ones. Run the diagnostic before rewriting a word, fix what is actually broken, and let the calendar handle the rest. As this site’s own Search Console fills with real impression data, I will update this article with which fixes moved which posts — that is the deal this site makes with its readers.
